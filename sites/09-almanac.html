A Word from the Scarecrow

I have stood in this field a long while. I have watched three neighbors who loved Halloween turn a hayride into something that crawls under the skin, and then hand the proceeds back to the town in cans and coats. I have watched the crows learn the actors' cues. I have watched grown men run.

This almanac is my account of what is coming this October. It is set down plain: the nights, the prices, the omens, the questions folk ask me at the gate. Read it by lantern. Then come and see whether I've told you true.

— The Scarecrow, from the crossbar on Harff Road

As the scarecrow reckons them. Consult the sky before you trust him.

Note well: the haunt runs dark on the 9th, 10th, 16th and 17th. The moon will be thin or absent every one of those nights. Bring your nerve; the corn will bring the dark.

III. Signs & Omens

What to expect on the trail, as the old folk would have told it.

When the wagon creaks…

The Hayride

It all begins with a hayride. You'll be carried out past the last porch light to where the trail starts, and the wagon will not turn around for anybody. Sit close. Keep your hands in.

When the corn whispers…

The Corn Maze

Rows taller than a tall man. Paths that fold back on themselves like a letter you'd rather not read. The old folk call it the Corn Field and speak of a Dark Harvest in it; something walks the rows beside you, keeping pace. It is probably one of ours.

When the trees close in…

The Haunted Trail

The trail winds through the forest by tunnels and winding paths: over the Bridge, through the Tunnel, past the Crossing and into the Last Woods. Keep to the path; the path does not always keep to you. What you hear behind you is closer than it sounds.

When a light burns in the barn…

The Haunted Buildings

Buildings full of live actors and displays: the Butcher's, the Saloon, the Bus that never left, the Barn Hang. Every door opens. Not every door lets you back out the way you came. If a room seems empty, it is waiting for you to say so out loud.

When a hand reaches from the dark…

Live Actors & Interactive Scares

Live actors, all through the trail, every one of them a neighbor who volunteered to be your nightmare: Beetlejuice at the yard, the Witch in the woods, whatever waits in the Blackout. This isn't just a haunt. It's an experience designed to crawl under your skin and stay there till Thanksgiving.

When the crows go quiet…

The Graveyard & the Cage

Ben builds the sets, and Ben built a zombie graveyard and a skeleton trapped in a cage. The skeleton has been in there some time. The crows won't sit near it, and crows will sit on anything.

Save Five Dollars — Bring a Tithe

What counts as a tithe

A tithe is nothing grander than a kindness carried in your hands. Bring one to the gate and keep five dollars in your pocket:

  • A can or box for the food shelf. Anything that keeps; the neighbors will carry it where it's needed.
  • A hat, a pair of gloves, or a coat. The winter clothing drive. It gets cold on the Crow; not everyone has a coat for it.

The other haunts around the Cities ask thirty to fifty dollars and won't tell you the number till checkout. This one is twenty, or fifteen and a kindness. The scarecrow approves.

V. For the Little Ones

One afternoon the scarecrow keeps the lights on.

Kids Day — Saturday, October 10
Noon–3 PM $5 a child $5 off an evening haunt 7:45 PM fireworks

By daylight the trail is a different creature. The lanterns stay lit, the actors keep their masks on their laps, and the little ones walk the lights-on trail, go trick-or-treating, and get up to family activities in the yard. Nobody jumps out. The scarecrow has given his word.

Hold on to your Kids Day ticket: it is good for $5 off an evening haunt ticket, for the grown-ups who'd like to come back after dark and find out what the corn is like without the sun.

Come back at dark for fireworks. On Saturday the 10th the sky over the field goes up at 7:45 PM, and the haunt runs 8:00 to 10:30 PM after it, last entry ten. The scarecrow will be watching the fireworks too; he says the crows hate them.

VI. The Three Neighbors

Why there is a haunt on Harff Road at all.

Three neighbors loved Halloween. Three neighbors loved giving back. They put the two together on a hayride, and the field has not been quiet in October since.

Every tithe carried through the gate goes where it's meant to. The haunt is a neighbor's project, built and staffed by neighbors, and it pays its debts to the town in the currency the town needs: food, warm clothes, and a good turn for the folk who run toward fires.

The Food Shelf

Every can you carry to the gate is stacked, counted and driven to the local food shelf. A cheaper ticket for you; a fuller shelf for a neighbor you may never meet.

The Clothing Drive

Hats, gloves, coats: the winter clothing drive gathers what a Minnesota November demands and hands it to folk who'd otherwise go without. Bring a warm thing; get in for fifteen.

Hanover Fire Department

The haunt partners with the Hanover Fire Department, the neighbors who show up when the night goes truly wrong. Part of what you spend on a scare goes back to the people who put fires out.

VIII. Questions Put to the Scarecrow

Asked at the gate, answered from the crossbar.

How scary is it, truly?

Scary enough that grown men have run past me faster than they walked in. There are live actors in the dark, tunnels, buildings you must go through, and a corn maze that does not care whether you find the way out. This isn't a hayride with a few pumpkins; it's built to crawl under your skin. If you frighten easily, come with someone whose sleeve you can hold.

How old should a child be before you'll let them in the corn after dark?

The night haunt is meant for teenagers and grown folk. It's dark, loud and full of people whose job is to startle you. For the little ones there is Kids Day on Saturday the 10th, noon till three, with the lights on and nobody jumping out. Parents know their own children; if in doubt, bring them by day first.

What if it rains, or worse?

A scarecrow does not mind weather, and a little rain makes the corn sound better. Dress for a Minnesota October: boots, a coat, a hat you don't mind losing. If the sky turns truly foul, the neighbors will say so on the socials that afternoon. Check there before you set out.

Where do I leave my wagon (or, as you call it, my car)?

Follow Harff Road to the lanterns and the folk in vests; they'll point you where to park at the field. Come a little early on Saturdays, when the line is longest, and bring your tithe in from the car so you don't have to walk back for it.

How long does the trail take?

That depends on the crowd ahead of you and how fast you run. Plan on the better part of an hour from the moment the hayride leaves, and don't arrive at the last minute: the gate admits nobody after ten o'clock.

Will the actors touch me? May I touch them?

Our actors won't lay hands on you. We ask the same courtesy of you: they're neighbors under those masks, and a scarecrow keeps a list. Keep your hands to yourself, leave the props where they are, and let the corn do its work.

Can a wheelchair or a stroller manage the trail?

I'll tell you true: the trail is dirt, roots and dark, winding through forest and corn, with tunnels and narrow paths. It's not friendly to wheels after dark. Kids Day, with the lights on, is the gentler afternoon. Write to the neighbors through the socials before you come and they'll tell you honestly what can be managed.

Is there anything to eat, or must I bring my own?

Two food trucks keep wagons at the field: Just in Time Concessions and Burger Box. Eat before the hayride or after; eating in the corn is not advised, for reasons the corn would rather show you.

IX. How to Find the Field

West of the Cities, on the far side of the Crow River.

The field lies at

6470 Harff Road
Greenfield, MN 55373

  1. Leave Minneapolis heading west; the drive is about thirty-five minutes, less if you're eager.
  2. Make for Greenfield, out in the west metro, near the Crow River. The river gave the haunt its name and the crows their opinions.
  3. Find Harff Road. Watch for the lanterns, the folk in vests and the line of cars that appears in October and nowhere else.
  4. Park where you're pointed. Bring your tithe. Get on the wagon. It all begins with a hayride.
